## A. Introduction: cooperation without commanding power

The ASEAN Way is often reduced to “not interfering.” Goh argues that this confuses a substantive principle—nonintervention—with a wider method of interaction: consultation, sensitivity, informal communication and nonconfrontational diplomacy. A state can apply pressure through diplomacy without using military force, so identifying action as “intervention” does not by itself establish that ASEAN abandoned every element of its diplomatic style. [S26, pp. 1–3, 6.]

Two further questions follow. Almonte asks how governments can turn improved relations into stronger security cooperation; Acharya asks whether ASEAN's role as a platform for wider regional institutions produces meaningful leadership. Together, these readings distinguish **diplomatic method**, **security outcomes** and **regional influence**. None is guaranteed simply by invoking ASEAN's name. [S20, pp. 1–4; S18, pp. 2–8.]

## B. Comprehensive lesson

### 1. Separate principles, procedures and results

| Analytical level | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Substantive principles | What should states respect? | Sovereignty, territorial integrity, noninterference, peaceful settlement |
| Diplomatic procedures | How should they interact? | Consultation, private dialogue, nonconfrontation, seeking agreement |
| Institutional arrangements | Where and through whom do they cooperate? | ASEAN bodies and ASEAN-led regional forums |
| Outcomes | What actually changes? | Greater trust, settlement, implementation—or continued disagreement |

Goh stresses that procedural norms prescribe the **means** of interaction rather than a particular policy end. Consultation describes how officials seek agreement; it does not tell us whether the final agreement protects rights, resolves a conflict or benefits every affected group. [S26, p. 2.]

This distinction prevents two opposite mistakes: assuming every instance of diplomatic pressure contradicts the entire ASEAN Way, and assuming polite diplomacy necessarily produces satisfactory results.

### 2. Goh: the ASEAN Way is more than nonintervention

Goh identifies a preference for agreement and harmony, sensitivity, politeness, nonconfrontation, quiet/private diplomacy and comparatively nonlegalistic interaction. She places these practices in a sociohistorical account of personal relations, elite networks and informal political authority. She also discusses consultation, or **musyawarah**, in earlier regional experiments. [S26, pp. 1–3.]

Treat the cultural explanation as **Goh's argument**, not proof that all Southeast Asian societies share a timeless political personality. Region-wide diversity and changes in legal institutions complicate such generalizations. The Charter, read in module 04, creates formal institutions and obligations while retaining consultation and consensus. It is therefore misleading to claim that ASEAN is simply without law because an earlier article emphasizes informality. [S26, pp. 2–3; S05, pp. 12–29.]

Goh's analytical intervention is narrower and more useful: **do not make nonintervention synonymous with every norm associated with ASEAN**. A documented breach of one principle may reveal inconsistency or selective practice; it does not automatically demonstrate the absence of consultation or diplomatic restraint. [S26, pp. 2, 6.]

### 3. The Cambodian conflict: active diplomacy without an ASEAN military alliance

Goh examines the Vietnamese intervention of late 1978 and the subsequent conflict involving Cambodian factions and foreign supporters. Cambodia, Vietnam and China were outside ASEAN at the time of the conflict's outbreak. This matters: ASEAN's response cannot be analyzed as though it were disciplining an existing member under the later Charter. [S26, pp. 3–4.]

Her account emphasizes ASEAN's early use of the United Nations, opposition to recognition of the Vietnamese-backed People's Republic of Kampuchea, calls for foreign troop withdrawal and diplomatic attempts to form a coalition among resistance factions. The means included pressure and international isolation, not passive silence. ASEAN did not turn itself into a military alliance against Vietnam. [S26, pp. 4–6.]

The example also has an ethical complication. Goh records ASEAN's support for retaining Democratic Kampuchea's UN representation despite Khmer Rouge atrocities. An answer that celebrates sovereignty diplomacy without acknowledging the representation issue misses the tension between strategic objectives and protection of people. Her positive assessment of ASEAN's diplomatic role should be evaluated alongside this evidence, rather than repeated as an unqualified moral verdict. [S26, pp. 4–6.]

**Do not infer:** “ASEAN resolved Cambodia alone.” Goh's own account includes Cambodian factions, external patrons and the UN. Nor does “ASEAN did not deploy an alliance army” mean the conflict itself was nonviolent. The distinction concerns ASEAN's chosen organizational instruments. [S26, pp. 4–6.]

### 4. Cambodia and Haiti: what Goh's comparison can and cannot show

Goh contrasts ASEAN's Cambodia diplomacy with the Organization of American States' response to the overthrow of Haiti's elected president, Jean-Bertrand Aristide. Her account describes OAS sanctions, direct diplomatic pressure, eventual coordination with the UN and the 1994 US military deployment preceding Aristide's restoration. [S26, pp. 4–6.]

| Comparison | Cambodia in Goh's account | Haiti in Goh's account |
|---|---|---|
| Membership context | Cambodia was not an ASEAN member at outbreak | Haiti was an OAS member |
| Political objective | Settlement among factions and foreign supporters; withdrawal issue | Restoration of the elected president |
| International channel | ASEAN engaged the UN early | OAS subsequently intensified UN cooperation |
| Coercive dimension | Diplomatic/economic isolation without an ASEAN military alliance | Sanctions and eventual military deployment in a wider international response |

Goh acknowledges that the conflicts differ. Consequently, the comparison illustrates contrasting methods but does **not** establish that ASEAN-style diplomacy is universally more effective or that coercion is always unnecessary. Different membership rules, power resources, conflict structures and objectives affect what instruments are available. [S26, pp. 3–6.]

Her discussion of Cambodia's delayed admission and election-related engagement in the late 1990s further shows why “noninterference means doing nothing” is too simple. Use the broad sequence—1997 crisis, delayed admission, mediation/election-related efforts, accession in 1999—rather than reproducing an unverified exact day from this article. [S26, p. 6; accession verified in S11, p. 4.]

### 5. Almonte: security cooperation must grow from political trust

Almonte identifies interconnected pressures toward cooperation: domestic conflicts capable of spilling across borders, terrorism, US–China strategic competition, unequal development and changing regional economic relationships. The reasoning is that national responses alone cannot fully manage interdependent risks; collective bargaining may also strengthen smaller states' influence. His terminology and forecasts belong to the **2004** setting, not a current intelligence assessment. [S20, pp. 1–2.]

Crucially, he does not assume that an ASEAN security community was already complete. He says describing interstate conflict as virtually unthinkable would still overstate the situation. He nevertheless regards the improvement from earlier confrontation as significant. Distinguish **progress toward peaceful relations** from **proof that war has become unthinkable**. [S20, p. 2.]

His explanation of “political will” adds a causal mechanism. Slow decisions do not arise only from leaders refusing to cooperate: confidence, solidarity and mutual trust affect their ability to make politically difficult commitments. Repeated practical cooperation can strengthen the basis for later cooperation. [S20, pp. 2–3.]

### 6. Proposals are not established institutions

Almonte discusses Indonesia's proposal for an ASEAN Peacekeeping Center by 2010 and a regional peacekeeping force by 2012. He also proposes developing the ASEAN Inter-Parliamentary Organization into a fuller regional parliament. These are **proposals and advocated goals**, not evidence that such institutions were created by those dates. [S20, pp. 2, 4.]

Likewise, the article's expectation of an East Asian Economic Grouping by 2010 must remain a historical forecast. The 2015 Reader publication date does not transform the underlying 2004 prediction into a verified retrospective result. [S20, pp. 1–2.]

The controlling primary-document distinction is that Bali Concord II explicitly says the ASEAN security community is **not a defense pact, military alliance or joint foreign policy**. Cooperation, national defense arrangements and discussion of possible peacekeeping mechanisms should not be collapsed into collective defense. [S03, pp. 3–4.]

### 7. Practical achievements link the three pillars

Almonte advocates improved counterterrorism coordination, maritime safety and common bargaining on economic questions. He also highlights social costs when border enforcement uproots people with long histories of cross-border movement. Security policy therefore affects livelihoods and social protection, not just defense agencies. [S20, p. 3.]

His proposed mechanisms illustrate a chain: **limited practical cooperation → experience of mutual benefit → greater trust/common purpose → capacity for deeper cooperation**. This is an argument about how community might be built, not an assurance that spillover always occurs. Distributional disputes or conflicting national interests may interrupt the process. [S20, pp. 2–4; final qualification is teaching synthesis.]

### 8. Acharya: what centrality actually means

Acharya's 2017 article distinguishes centrality from the simple claim that ASEAN is important. Its narrow institutional meaning is ASEAN's position as the platform for wider arrangements such as **ASEAN Plus Three (APT), the ASEAN Regional Forum (ARF), and the East Asia Summit (EAS)**. Broader uses portray ASEAN as a hub of debates about regional norms or even as a model for other organizations. These are different claims, requiring different evidence. [S18, pp. 2–3.]

A major corrective is that centrality was not created by ASEAN alone. External powers also accepted ASEAN-led processes because they trusted one another less and found an inclusive ASEAN platform comparatively acceptable. Centrality therefore depends on both ASEAN agency and the surrounding distribution of interests and power. [S18, pp. 2–5.]

**Strategic purpose:** preserving ASEAN's relevance and members' room for maneuver. **Normative mechanism:** inclusive, comparatively nonconstraining forums make participation less threatening and may promote dialogue, confidence-building and restraint. The same limited constraints that facilitate participation may also limit enforcement. [S18, pp. 4–5; last sentence is synthesis.]

### 9. Centrality of form versus centrality of substance

Drawing on Surin Pitsuwan, Acharya argues that centrality must be earned. Being at the center of meeting arrangements is **form**; setting agendas, giving direction and resolving disagreements are **substance**. [S18, p. 7.]

| Evidence | What it supports | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Major powers attend an ASEAN-led forum | Convening/platform role | ASEAN controls their policies |
| Joint statements are issued | Some agreement on language | Commitments are implemented |
| Members coordinate on a dispute | Collective diplomatic capacity | Differences have permanently disappeared |
| A policy initiative produces a verified result | Substantive effectiveness in that case | Success on every other security issue |

This evidence ladder is a teaching framework derived from the form/substance distinction, not a quoted classification from Acharya.

### 10. Acharya's four challenges—in their 2017 setting

1. **Diminishing internal cohesion.** He highlights the failure to issue a joint communiqué at the 2012 Phnom Penh ministerial meeting, institutional burdens, national interests and regime-security considerations. Disunity cannot be blamed solely on an external power. [S18, pp. 5–6.]
2. **Erosion of neutrality.** A broker's credibility weakens when members or external powers increasingly align the regional process with great-power rivalry. [S18, p. 6.]
3. **Parallel and China-centered regional initiatives.** ASEAN-led institutions coexist with other arrangements, including the AIIB and the initiative called OBOR in the article. Their emergence complicates any assumption that all regional cooperation must pass through ASEAN. [S18, pp. 6–7.]
4. **Pressures on the US-led liberal order.** Acharya discusses the first Trump administration's preference for bilateral deals and potential consequences for open regionalism. This is a 2017 analysis and forecast, **not** a verified account of subsequent years. [S18, p. 7.]

His conclusion is deliberately differentiated: one can value ASEAN's contribution to Southeast Asian cooperation while doubting its capacity to sustain an expansive centrality claim. The association's continued relevance is not logically identical to its leadership of the entire Asia-Pacific architecture. Unity and neutrality are central to his account of what credible centrality requires. [S18, p. 8.]

## C. Terms and distinctions

- **ASEAN Way:** a contested label for norms and diplomatic practices associated with ASEAN. In Goh's account, its procedural elements are broader than nonintervention.
- **Noninterference/nonintervention:** restraint concerning other states' domestic affairs; do not treat the terms as resolving every legal or political boundary question.
- **Musyawarah:** consultation, discussed by Goh in the history of regional interaction.
- **Quiet diplomacy:** discreet communication and negotiation rather than public confrontation; not necessarily inaction.
- **Conflict management:** containing and handling a dispute; it need not remove all underlying causes.
- **Conflict resolution:** seeking a settlement of the dispute; a stronger claim than keeping dialogue open.
- **Security community:** peaceful relations and expectations of peaceful change; not synonymous with a collective-defense alliance. Almonte cautions against assuming the strongest peaceful expectation was already established.
- **Collective defense:** an alliance-type commitment concerning defense against attack; not the model established by Concord II's security-community declaration.
- **Centrality:** ASEAN's anchoring/driving role in wider regional processes; distinguish institutional position from effective influence.
- **Cohesion:** ability of members to maintain a coordinated position; not internal uniformity.
- **Neutrality:** in Acharya's discussion, avoiding taking sides in great-power rivalry; not a claim that members have no interests or bilateral relationships.

[Sources: S26, pp. 1–6; S20, pp. 1–4; S18, pp. 2–8; S03, pp. 3–4. Definitions of management/resolution and collective defense are teaching distinctions.]

## E. Cause and effect

1. Postcolonial sensitivity to autonomy → diplomatic reassurance through sovereignty and restraint → broader willingness to cooperate → difficulty accepting intrusive regional authority.
2. Spillover risks and economic interdependence → demand for regional coordination → practical common projects → possible growth in trust, but not automatic political merger.
3. Great powers distrust each other → ASEAN-led forums provide an acceptable platform → ASEAN gains a convening role → external cooperation and internal cohesion remain conditions for influence.
4. Divergent national/regime interests → weaker common positions → reduced agenda-setting credibility → centrality of form may survive while centrality of substance weakens.

[Interpretive synthesis of S26, S20 and S18; the arrows describe mechanisms, not deterministic laws.]

## F. Connections to other lessons

- **Country histories:** Konfrontasi and Sabah help explain why developing trust was itself an achievement. Regime security also explains why governments may resist scrutiny.
- **Institutions:** Charter consensus can preserve acceptance while making a coordinated external position harder. Legal personality alone does not solve this political problem.
- **Human rights:** whose security is protected—states, governing elites or people? The diplomatic defense of sovereignty may conflict with individual protection; further assigned human-security and rights readings remain pending.
- **Economics:** bargaining strength and unequal development link integration to security. Monetary cooperation requires its own evidence; it cannot be inferred from a security-community proposal.

## G. Worked examples

### Hypothetical spillover crisis

A domestic conflict displaces people across borders. Governments may consult privately, agree on relief coordination and involve international agencies while rejecting a regional military intervention. This is **active cooperation using restrained instruments**, not necessarily abandonment of every ASEAN norm. Whether the response adequately protects people is a separate evaluative question. This is a teaching scenario, not a reported historical operation.

### Evidence-based paragraph

**Prompt:** “Does the ASEAN Way make ASEAN powerless?”

**Model answer:** Not necessarily. Goh distinguishes nonintervention from consultation and nonconfrontational diplomatic methods; her Cambodia case records active UN diplomacy and pressure without an ASEAN military alliance (S26, pp. 2, 4–6). These instruments can influence conflicts despite limited enforcement capacity. However, the case does not establish universal success: it involved external powers and the UN, and the representation of Democratic Kampuchea exposed an ethical tension. Acharya further shows that convening regional forums does not guarantee substantive leadership when members lack cohesion (S18, pp. 5–8). ASEAN has meaningful instruments, but their effectiveness is conditional.

## H. Takeaways, misconceptions and self-test

**Remember:** method ≠ principle ≠ outcome; cooperation ≠ alliance; proposal ≠ institution; centrality of form ≠ centrality of substance.

**Avoid:**

- “Noninterference means ASEAN never acts.”
- “ASEAN alone brought peace to Cambodia.”
- “The OAS itself was the US force deployed in Haiti.”
- “ASEAN created the proposed peacekeeping force by 2012.”
- “The 2015 Reader's republication makes Almonte's 2004 forecasts verified facts.”
- “Acharya says ASEAN has no value.”
- “Great powers attending ASEAN-led meetings proves ASEAN controls them.”
- “Cultural explanations establish that all Southeast Asians behave alike.”

**Self-test:**

1. How can diplomatic pressure coexist with a nonconfrontational method?
2. Why does Cambodia's membership status at conflict outbreak matter?
3. What limits the Cambodia–Haiti comparison?
4. How does Almonte explain political will through political strength?
5. Why might major powers accept leadership by materially weaker states?
6. What evidence would demonstrate centrality of substance rather than form?
7. Can ASEAN remain relevant if its wider centrality weakens? Explain Acharya's answer.
