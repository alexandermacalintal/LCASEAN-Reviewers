## Part II — Correct the claim

**Rubric:** 1 point for identifying the error, 1 for correction, 1 for explanation.

16. **TAC is 1976, not 1967.** The first ASEAN Summit and TAC belong to the Bali consolidation of 1976; 1967 is ASEAN's founding year. The slide contains an error, so use primary/supporting documentary corroboration. [Concord II, p. 1; Overview, p. 14.]
17. **Signature and entry into force differ.** Charter signed 20 November 2007; effective 15 December 2008. A signed agreement need not take effect immediately. [Charter, p. 42; Overview, pp. 5, 15.]
18. **Nonintervention is not the whole ASEAN Way.** Goh records pressure and UN diplomacy over Cambodia while distinguishing nonconfrontational procedure from abstention. Explain the instrument used before labeling the organization inactive. [S26, pp. 2, 4–6.]
19. **Attendance does not prove control.** External powers may accept a forum for their own reasons. Demonstrating substantive leadership requires evidence about agendas, direction, decisions and outcomes, not just participation. [S18, pp. 2–5, 7–8.]
20. **A target is an aspiration/commitment, not an outcome measure.** Verify implementation against evidence. The same caution applies to Almonte's proposed institutions and dates. [Vision 2025, pp. 3–7; S20, pp. 2, 4.]

## Part III — Short answers

### 21. Sovereignty and historical context

Newly independent states confronted territorial consolidation, insurgencies, national development, relations with former colonial powers and major-power competition. Sovereign equality and noninterference offered reassurance that cooperation would not reproduce external domination or authorize easy intrusion into fragile domestic orders. These principles can make regional membership politically acceptable. They can also constrain a collective response when harms originate inside national borders.

**Award:** historical tasks (2), mechanism linking them to sovereignty (2), trade-off (1). Avoid claiming every government had identical motives. [Weatherbee, pp. 7–9, 13–22; modules 01–03.]

### 22. Institutional functions

The **Summit** sets supreme political direction; the **ACC** coordinates across pillars and prepares summits; **Community Councils** coordinate within their respective pillars; the **SG/Secretariat** facilitates, monitors and administers within its mandate. Sectoral bodies and national agencies carry out specialized work. These are complementary levels, not interchangeable names for a regional executive government.

**Award:** four correct roles (4), distinction between political authority and administrative support (1). [Charter, pp. 14–23.]

### 23. Consensus

Consensus can protect smaller states and increase acceptance of a joint position; it may also produce delay, watered-down language or failure to agree. Minus X permits economic implementation flexibility where members agree to use it, allowing ready participants to proceed without making this a general authorization to impose any policy on dissenters. Article 20 also preserves qualifications to the basic consensus principle.

**Award:** benefit (1), limitation (1), explanation (1), conditional economic flexibility (2). [Charter, pp. 26–27; module 04.]

### 24. Comparison limits

Cambodia and Haiti differed in membership status, conflict structure, foreign involvement, objectives and coercive resources. In Goh's account, Cambodia involved factions and external patrons while Haiti concerned restoration of an elected president after a coup. ASEAN's early UN diplomacy contrasts with the OAS response and later international military role, but those differences do not isolate one diplomatic method as the cause of success. Do not equate the OAS as an organization with the US troops deployed.

**Award:** two concrete differences (2), methods contrasted accurately (1), causal/ethical limitation (2). [S26, pp. 3–6.]

### 25. Trust and political will

Almonte argues that political will depends on political strength grounded in solidarity and mutual trust. Earlier conflict made extensive cooperation politically difficult. Practical joint achievements may generate a sense of common purpose and create support for further cooperation. This is a proposed mechanism, not a guarantee that every small project causes deeper integration or that ASEAN had already achieved a complete security community.

**Award:** political-strength link (2), practical-achievement mechanism (2), qualification (1). [S20, pp. 2–4.]

### 26. Four challenges in Acharya

1. Weaker intra-ASEAN cohesion, including the 2012 communiqué failure and domestic/regime interests.
2. Erosion of neutrality amid great-power rivalry.
3. China's parallel and increasingly China-centered regional initiatives alongside ASEAN-led forums.
4. Pressure on the US-led liberal order, including the first Trump administration's bilateral preferences.

**Award:** four challenges (4), explicit recognition of the article's 2017 perspective/forecasts (1). Later events require separate verification; the answer must not present a forecast as a documented later outcome. [S18, pp. 5–8.]

## Part IV — Applied questions

### 27. Emergency response

A strong answer identifies the conflict between sovereign control and cross-border humanitarian effects. Neighboring governments have reasons to cooperate because displacement, health risks and livelihoods do not remain within one border. Consultation, agreed relief arrangements, mediation and engagement with international agencies may be active responses even without military intervention. Their adequacy for protecting people remains an independent question.

The Summit may provide political direction; relevant sectoral agencies undertake specialist work; Community Councils coordinate their pillars; the ACC supports cross-pillar coherence; the Secretariat facilitates and monitors within mandate. Domestic implementation still relies on national agencies. The SG does not acquire an automatic power to command national forces or override a government merely because the problem is urgent.

Public criticism, private diplomacy, humanitarian cooperation and coercive intervention are different instruments, not an all-or-nothing choice. Evidence of meetings alone does not demonstrate successful protection; examine access, delivery and outcomes. This is an application, not a claim that the Charter prescribes a fixed response sequence.

**Rubric:** sovereignty/spillover tension (3); accurate institutions and limits (3); instruments distinguished (2); evidence/outcomes distinction (2). [Charter, pp. 14–29; S26, pp. 1–6; S20, p. 3.]

### 28. Centrality inference

Hosting demonstrates a convening/platform role. It does not show that participants changed policy or that rivalry disappeared. Acharya's distinction between centrality of form and substance requires additional evidence: who set the agenda, whether members maintained a coherent position, whether external powers accepted direction, and whether a specific disagreement was addressed in a verifiable way.

External acceptance matters because major powers may prefer an ASEAN-led forum to one led by a rival; they are not necessarily subordinating themselves. Internal cohesion matters because disagreement among ASEAN states can prevent meaningful regional direction. Acharya also argues that an ASEAN still useful within Southeast Asia could lose some wider centrality. Do not infer either complete success or total irrelevance from a single meeting.

**Rubric:** identifies invalid inference (2); form/substance distinction (2); additional evidence (2); external acceptance (2); internal cohesion and nuanced conclusion (2). [S18, pp. 2–8.]

## Part V — Essay

### 29. Model argument and outline

**Thesis:** Sovereignty safeguards helped diverse and vulnerable states accept cooperation; the same safeguards limit how far regional bodies can demand concessions. Institutional development changes the mechanisms of cooperation without automatically ending this tension.

1. **History:** Use a documented case such as Indonesia's shift from Konfrontasi toward regional cooperation, or Philippine concerns about foreign bases and autonomy. Explain why the experience makes reassurance important; do not merely list events. [Module 02 and its country-specific references; S20, p. 2.]
2. **Rules:** Charter Article 3 is intergovernmental; Article 5 requires domestic implementation; institutions clarify functions; consultation/consensus remain basic. The Charter also contains dispute procedures and qualifications to consensus. [Charter, pp. 12–29.]
3. **Mechanism:** Consultation can lower fears of domination and sustain participation; conflicting national preferences can block or dilute common action.
4. **Reading/application:** Goh's Cambodia case demonstrates diplomatic action without an ASEAN military alliance, while representation politics exposes ethical costs. Alternatively, Almonte explains why trust supports stronger cooperation, or Acharya connects cohesion to substantive centrality. [S26, pp. 3–6; S20, pp. 2–4; S18, pp. 5–8.]
5. **Counterargument:** Sovereignty does not mean every commitment is optional. Governments may voluntarily accept obligations, and agreed cooperation or flexibility can enable collective action without a superstate.
6. **Conclusion:** Evaluate particular commitments, instruments and verified outcomes. Neither “ASEAN is powerless” nor “community-building is complete” follows from the available evidence.

**Rubric (20):** clear qualified thesis (3); historically grounded case (4); accurate institutions/rules (4); scholarly evidence and mechanism (4); counterargument (3); clear separation of aspiration, interpretation and outcome (2).

## Revision advice

For incorrect answers, return to the named module or physical source pages rather than memorizing the correct letter. A good short answer connects a definition to a mechanism and example, then states its limit. Questions about unread resources must be added when those readings are actually processed.
