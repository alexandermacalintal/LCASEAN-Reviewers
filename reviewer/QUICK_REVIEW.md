## 1. The organizing question

**How can diverse sovereign states cooperate without becoming a single state?** ASEAN combines national autonomy with common institutions, consultation and commitments. Its achievements and limits must both be explained.

- **Regional cooperation:** working jointly on common concerns.
- **Regional integration:** closer connections and shared rules; not necessarily political merger.
- **Intergovernmental:** governments retain central decision authority. Charter Article 3 explicitly uses this model.
- **Legal personality:** ASEAN exists as a legal entity; this does not establish supranational supremacy.
- **Centrality:** ASEAN's intended coordinating/driving role in regional architecture, not military dominance.

[Charter, pp. 7–13, 34–37; modules 01, 03–04.]

## 3. Why sovereignty matters

Decolonization, weak state institutions, insurgency, territorial disputes and great-power intervention made autonomy politically important. Noninterference reassures governments and enables cooperation; it can also constrain responses to domestic abuses or transboundary crises. Do not reduce ASEAN's founding to either trade alone or anticommunism alone. The Bangkok Declaration includes development, peace, culture, research and technical cooperation. [Bangkok Declaration, pp. 1–3; Weatherbee, pp. 7–9, 13–22.]

**Four theoretical lenses:** realism emphasizes power and interests; liberal integrationism examines interdependence and institution-building; regime theory examines shared rules and expectations; constructivism examines norms and socially formed identities. These are competing explanatory approaches, not four separate ASEAN institutions. [Weatherbee, pp. 19–22.]

## 4. Three pillars — know the differences and connections

| Pillar | Main concerns | Exam trap |
|---|---|---|
| APSC | Peace, political cooperation, security, peaceful settlement | Not a military alliance or joint foreign policy |
| AEC | Connected markets/production, trade, investment and economic cooperation | Not proof of a common currency or unrestricted migration |
| ASCC | Education, health, inclusion, protection, identity, environment and disasters | Not merely cultural performances |

Concord II originally called its security pillar **ASC**; later Charter-era terminology is **APSC**. A product crossing borders needs peaceful relations, economic arrangements and capable/protected workers: the pillars reinforce one another. [Concord II, pp. 2–7; Charter, pp. 16–17.]

## 5. Institutional roles — memorize the function

- **Summit:** heads of state/government; supreme policymaking.
- **ACC:** foreign ministers; cross-pillar coordination and summit preparation.
- **Community Councils:** coordination within each of the three pillars.
- **Sectoral bodies:** specialized implementation.
- **Secretary-General/Secretariat:** administration, facilitation and monitoring; not a regional government.
- **CPR:** permanent ambassador-level representation in Jakarta.
- **National secretariats:** national coordination and implementation links.
- **Chair:** annual rotating leadership; not the Secretary-General.
- **Secretary-General:** nonrenewable **five-year** term.
- **AICHR:** human-rights body; do not assume a court with binding individual judgments.

[Charter, Arts. 7–15, pp. 14–23; Arts. 31–32, pp. 31–32.]

## 6. Decision-making and settlement

**Consensus** is the basic rule, not ordinary majority voting. It can build acceptance and protect smaller members but also delay or dilute decisions. Article 20 contains qualifications; Article 21 permits **ASEAN Minus X for economic commitments where there is consensus to use it**. It is not unrestricted permission to outvote opponents.

ASEAN **does have dispute-settlement provisions**: dialogue, consultation, negotiation, agreed mediation/good offices, instrument-specific procedures and referral of unresolved matters to the Summit. Political referral is not an automatic penalty. [Charter, pp. 26–29.]

## 8. High-value answer formula

**Define → identify mechanism → give a documented example → state the limitation.**

Example: “ASEAN is intergovernmental (Charter Art. 3). The Charter creates organs and implementation obligations, but governments retain central authority and consensus remains basic. Legalization therefore strengthens cooperation without automatically creating a supranational government.”

## 9. Corrections and traps

1. TAC is **1976**, not the Structures slide's erroneous 1967.
2. The Overview filename says 2012, but its internal version is **16 February 2010**.
3. A **vision/blueprint is a commitment**, not evidence its targets were achieved.
4. Historical ten-member lists and population figures are not verified current rosters/statistics.
5. Secretariat departments are not Community Councils.
6. Oxfam/December 2009 presentation critiques must be attributed, not repeated as timeless neutral facts.
7. A map may include nonmembers and does not settle disputed boundaries.

## 10. ASEAN Way and security — three readings

| Reading | Main point | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| **Gillian Goh (2003)** | ASEAN Way procedures—consultation, private diplomacy, nonconfrontation—are broader than nonintervention | Diplomatic pressure is not necessarily abandonment of the entire method; Cambodia/Haiti comparison does not prove universal superiority |
| **Jose T. Almonte (2004; reprinted 2015)** | Practical cooperation builds common purpose; political will depends on trust/solidarity | Peacekeeping center/force and parliament are proposals, not verified institutions; forecasts remain historical |
| **Amitav Acharya (2017)** | Centrality depends on ASEAN and external powers; institutional position differs from effective leadership | ASEAN can remain valuable even if wider centrality weakens |

**Form versus substance:** hosting ASEAN-led forums demonstrates a platform role; agenda-setting, direction and resolving disagreements demonstrate stronger substantive influence. Major powers may accept an ASEAN platform because they distrust one another—not because ASEAN controls them.

**Acharya's four challenges (2017):** weaker internal cohesion; erosion of neutrality; China's parallel/China-centered regional initiatives; pressures on the US-led liberal international order. Do not present 2017 forecasts as verified later events.

**Cambodia:** ASEAN used UN diplomacy and pressure without forming an ASEAN military alliance. Cambodia was not a member at the initial outbreak. Khmer Rouge representation at the UN complicates an unqualified success narrative. Do not claim ASEAN acted alone or that the conflict itself was nonviolent.

[S26, pp. 1–6; S20, pp. 1–4; S18, pp. 2–8; full explanation: module 05.]
